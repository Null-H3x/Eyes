How to read this

Why the drift-0 skeleton is demoted. A 25-character English window carries ~23 repeated-letter pairs; the ciphertext forces only ~5. No natural language can match that at drift-0 — it's structurally impossible, which is why every vowel-heavy word "breaks." Under drift ±1 the plaintext's repeats are spread across positions and produce no ciphertext coincidence, so language becomes viable. The zero adjacent doubles in the corpus point the same way. So the red cells above are expected; the headline is the drift ±1 line.

Consistency is necessary, not sufficient. Under drift ±1 the check is permissive — most guesses pass. That's stage one of three: (1) here, (2) the same author-voice guess at a second target with the shared symbols agreeing, (3) cascade — does the implied alphabet decrypt the rest of the corpus coherently. Only cascade is decisive.

Wildcards. . imposes no constraint at its position — assert only the letters you're confident of and leave the rest free. Good for partial known-plaintext.

Case, digits, punctuation. Input is case-sensitive and accepts digits and punctuation. The alphabet size, 83 = 26 + 26 + 10 + 21, exactly fits a full case-sensitive character set — so upper/lower/digits/symbols may genuinely be in the plaintext. A case-sensitive guess also carries fewer repeats, which fits the sparse ciphertext better.

Coverage ceiling. The four targets reach 75/83 symbols. Eight — 27, 28, 35, 39, 51, 52, 74, 76 — sit in no same-plaintext structure and can't be pinned by any crib; they need an external anchor or a cascade that reaches them.

Caveat. This apparatus is valid to the extent the linear/sliding family holds. If good full-passage guesses never cascade, that is itself evidence the model is wrong (a clock, not a ramp) — the tool is a probe as much as a lever.
